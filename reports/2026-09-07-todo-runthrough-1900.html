gfx906 Tuning Run-Through

Ten open questions from the tuning guide's TODO list, measured on the four Vega 20 dies in one day, with the two interruptions that turned into a finding about the machine's power supply.

What changed in the guide. Two tensor-split pairs replace one four-die server from 8 streams up on the stock build; on the production build one four-die server at 16 slots takes that row back. A one-line kernel patch removes the 9-to-16-slot cliff on the split, and a rewrite of the vector kernel's Q8_0 path adds +61% at 12 slots and +36% at 16 on top, +36% at 8 slots on one die. A 4-bit value cache now runs and lets eight 256K contexts fit at no quality cost. The ML-gfx906 fork's tile table reads prompts a third faster, and one binary now carries it with the kernel rewrite at identical perplexity: the production build. Q4_0 is a measured quality loss, Q6_K a capacity trade. Nothing in the environment moves single-stream decode. And the "clock clamp" is the Mac Pro's 1228 W DC envelope, cleared only by a cold power cycle; a 175 W per-die cap keeps serving within 2% and the machine inside it.

Giving the short traffic its own pair takes it from 0.9 to 8.1 tok/s and from 318 s to 90 s of wall time. The long request pays: first token at 476 s instead of 262. 9. A 16-column vector kernel stair removed on the split

The stock build routes batches of 9 to 16 to a 16-wide tile kernel, which is why the guide said to sit at 8 or 16 slots. One constant and its template instantiations extend the vector kernel to 16 columns. Correct on 1186 of 1186 matrix-multiply tests.

On the split the stair is gone and 12 slots is the new peak. On a single die the opposite: the 16-column kernel needs 113 registers, leaves two waves per SIMD, and loses to the tile on a full-width matrix. So the patched build is for tensor-split servers wanting 9 to 16 slots; single-die instances keep the stock build at 8. The optimiser models both builds with a --build flag; with the patch the team-chat profile moves to 12 slots at 173 tok/s.

Static instruction counts and hardware counters agree: the eight-column kernel is not issue-bound. Its SIMDs are 30% busy; the idle 70% is load latency, 96 loads per wave behind a 15-deep wait chain at the four-waves-per-SIMD occupancy that 64 registers allow. The code target is occupancy and load pipelining, not instruction count, with a bound of roughly 2× on the eight-slot step. The memory-side counters were not obtained: the profiler aborts at process exit after its second counter pass.

10c. Rewriting the vector kernel +62% at 12 slots, +37% on one die

Started at 11:47. Two compile-time knobs on the kernel's gfx906 table (rows per thread block, warps per block) and a Q8_0 fast path that loads each weight block's quants and scale once per row and each activation block's once per column, applying the two scales as one product per pair. The occupancy hypothesis from the counters was wrong as a lever: halving registers per thread doubles the waves in flight and loses 20 to 35%, because each loaded activation block then feeds half as many rows. The kernel is bound by load instructions per dot product. Two things follow from that and both won: fewer instructions between the loads and the accumulate (the fast path, the tensor-split winner) and more rows per loaded block (rows 4, the single-die winner at +36% for batch 8). Iteration 3 tested the load-count reading directly and refuted it: loading a whole 32-weight block per thread, a quarter of the load instructions per dot product, loses 16 to 32% at batch 8 because the dependent dot-product chain per thread becomes four times longer; it wins only at batch 1 (+5% on one die). Rows 8 loses to register pressure, and the fast path reproduces to 0.3%. Iteration 4 then shared one LDS-staged copy of each iteration's activations across the warps of a block and lost 16 to 52%, so activation re-read traffic is not the binding resource either. What four iterations agree on: fewer instructions per dot product wins, anything that adds a barrier or lengthens a dependent chain loses, and registers beyond about 64 per thread lose. Iteration 5 confirmed it: the hybrid of rows 4 up to 8 columns and rows 2 above is one binary that matches the best of both earlier winners on every cell, +8% at 8 slots, +62% at 12 and +36% at 16 on the split, +14% at 4 and +37% at 8 slots on one die, and it is now the guide's serving build. The aligned-dword weight load lost 9 to 17% with a parity branch and, made branchless, still loses 10 to 16% at 12 and 16 columns where registers are critical, so it is dropped; the hybrid build reproduced a fifth time. Fed to the optimiser, the new kernel moves the team and busy-server profiles back to the four-die split at 16 slots (204 and 197 tok/s, beating the two pairs' 179) and offline batch to 282 tok/s on four single-die instances; the server-level confirmation is the next measurement.

The clamp was the power supply

Twice in the morning all four dies dropped to 1000 MHz and stayed there through idle, perf-level changes and a driver reset, invalidating two runs. The cold-cycled box ran a four-phase power test with the SMC's own budget table logged every five seconds.

phaseloadDC total WAC W (meter)CPU zone Wresult
0idle302358371730 MHz
1rocBLAS single-die prefill, the morning's suspect54867584no clamp
2four dies at the 200 W cap, host idle1062135388no clamp
3the same plus a full CPU load12641428303clamped in 20 s, latched

The DC envelope is 1228 W. Four loaded dies with the host idle sit 166 W inside it; any host load above about 150 W beside them crosses it, and the SMC clamps the GPUs, hive-wide, until a cold power cycle. A warm reboot brings the clocks back but not the SMC, which is why the second clamp came five minutes after a reboot at 550 W. The rules since: host CPU capped at 150 W by every benchmark script, no host work beside GPU jobs, a watchdog that stops the chain when the DC total nears the envelope, and a cold cycle after any clamp. The 06:47 clamp, three and a half hours into a chain with no power log, is the one event the envelope does not fully explain.

Decode never reaches the cap: the four dies draw 130 to 170 W each while serving, so only prefill pays for a lower cap.
